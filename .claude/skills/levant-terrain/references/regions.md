## 0. The frame: three gradients and four floras

Almost every look in the region falls out of three gradients. Get these right and a map
reads as Levantine even with generic assets.

1. **Rain falls from north to south and from west to east.**
   - The north of Israel gets more than 900 mm a year. Jerusalem gets 525 mm, Beersheba
     193 mm and Eilat 24 mm [S1][S2].
   - More than 70% of the rain falls from November to March, and June to September are
     usually rainless [S2].
   - Every eastern slope sits in a rain shadow. The Judean Desert gets about 100 mm in
     the west, falling to 50 mm near the Dead Sea [S12]. The Beqaa is drier than the
     Lebanese coast [S51], and the Syrian steppe is drier than the Anti-Lebanon [S16].
2. **Rock is pale and calcareous in the west, dark basalt in the north-east, and red
   sandstone and granite in the far south.**
   - Most of Israel, Lebanon and western Jordan is limestone, dolomite, chalk and marl.
   - The Golan, the Hauran and the Harrat al-Sham are basalt [S24][S27][S46].
   - Eilat, southern Sinai and Wadi Rum are Precambrian granite under Nubian sandstone
     [S35][S40][S45].
3. **Ground is green only in winter, everywhere outside irrigation.**
   - The annual vegetation greens with the winter rain, and its satellite greenness peak
     lags the rain [S67]. It dries to straw in the long rainless summer [S2].
   - Only irrigated land and evergreen woody plants stay green all year: citrus, bananas,
     fishponds, olives, oak maquis and planted pine.

**The four phytogeographic regions** are a standard frame for which plants belong where
[S62]:
- **Mediterranean:** the coast and hills with more than about 350 mm of rain (oak, pine,
  maquis).
- **Irano-Turanian:** steppe belts of roughly 150-350 mm (dwarf shrubs, *Artemisia*).
- **Saharo-Arabian:** true desert of less than about 150 mm (very sparse shrubs, acacia in
  wadis).
- **Sudanian penetration:** hot rift oases such as the Dead Sea and the Arava, with
  *Ziziphus*, *Acacia*, *Salvadora* and tropical elements [S62].

The rainfall thresholds in that list are approximate (est.). Sources give 200-300 mm for
the Mediterranean/steppe limit [S62] and 150-250 mm for the steppe band [S47].

## 1. Mediterranean coastal plain

**Where.** A strip along the sea from Gaza to Lebanon. It is about 40 km wide at Gaza and
narrows to about 5 km at the Lebanese border [S1]. In Lebanon it is a discontinuous ribbon,
often only a few km wide, under the mountains (est.).

**Climate.**
- Köppen class:
  - Csa (hot-summer Mediterranean) in Israel and Lebanon [S2].
  - BSh (hot semi-arid) in Gaza [S5].
- Rainfall:
  - Tel Aviv 583 mm and Haifa 542 mm [S2].
  - Gaza 225 mm in the south to 400 mm in the north, nearly all from November to
    February [S5].
  - Beirut 700-900 mm. Sources range from 688 to 1,103 mm, and the most common values are
    726 and 850 mm [S52].
- Summer is humid and hazy at the coast (see section 15).

**Relief and landforms.**
- The plain is almost flat. Its relief comes from **kurkar ridges**: two or three
  coast-parallel ridges of calcareous aeolian sandstone (kurkar), interleaved with red
  palaeosols called *hamra* [S4][S5][S6].
- The sea cliff that cuts the coastal ridge (the Sharon escarpment) is up to 45 m high and
  75-90° steep [S6]. Inland ridges are lower (est. 10-40 m).
- Swampy troughs lay behind the ridges, where the ridges dammed the drainage [S6]. Most of
  them are drained now, with eucalyptus planted to help (see below) [S66].
- Gaza's highest point is only 105 m [S5]. It has three kurkar ridges with
  alluvium-filled wadis between them, and dunes near the coast [S5].
- The **Wadi Gaza** crosses the strip. Coastal dune belts are best developed in the south:
  Ashdod, Nitzanim, the Gaza coast (al-Mawasi), and on into north Sinai (section 7) (est.).
- Relief over 1-5 km is usually 10-60 m (est.).

**Geology and surface colour.**
- Kurkar is a buff to ochre calcarenite with honeycomb (tafoni) weathering (est.).
- Hamra is red to reddish-brown, iron-rich and silty [S7].
- Beach and dune sand is pale cream to light yellow, a quartz-carbonate mix (est.).
- Alluvium in the troughs is dark brown and grey (est.).
- North of the Carmel the coast becomes chalk and limestone headlands. Rosh HaNikra's
  white chalk cliff and grottoes are the type example [S1].

**Soils.**
- Hamra (red sandy loam) runs from Ashdod to the Carmel coast [S7].
- Sand (regosol) lies near the sea.
- Dark alluvial clays lie in the troughs (est.).

**Vegetation.**
- **Wild** cover is almost gone: urban land, orchards and fields. Coastal sands carry
  sparse dune shrubs. *Retama* and *Artemisia monosperma* are typical; the species are my
  general knowledge, not checked here.
- **Planted trees** define the look:
  - Citrus groves: Gaza lists citrus as a primary export [S5], and Israel's coastal plain
    has citrus orchards [S1].
  - **Eucalyptus** (*E. camaldulensis*), introduced in the 1880s and planted in groves
    around 1890-1900 to drain the Hadera swamps [S66]. It stands in rows and groves, with
    pale bark.
  - **Date palms**: Deir al-Balah is named the "monastery of the date palm" and has vast
    groves [S89].
  - **Sycamore fig** and **olive** (Gaza's agricultural products include olives [S5]).
  - **Prickly pear (sabra) hedges** along old field and village boundaries [S65].
- **Greenhouses:** Gaza had about 1,305 ha of greenhouses before the 2023 war [S76]. White
  plastic tunnel and greenhouse roofs are one of the strongest aerial signatures of the
  southern coastal plain. The same is true of the Israeli western Negev edge, though I
  found no area figure for it.
- **Season:** the field margins are green from December to April, then straw. The citrus
  and eucalyptus stay dark green, which gives the irrigated plain a summer contrast of
  dark trees on pale ground (est.).

**Water.**
- The Mediterranean, with a sandy shore and kurkar abrasion platforms (est.).
- Small coastal streams, now mostly drains.
- The Wadi Gaza [S5].

**Human landscape.**
- This is the densest-built region in the study.
  - The Gaza Strip is 365 km² for about 2.05 million people, about 5,600-6,000 per km²
    [S5].
  - Its eight refugee camps are much denser still. More than 620,000 people lived on less
    than 6.5 km² before the war, about 95,000 per km² [S75]. Jabalia is about 1.4 km² with
    about 110,000 people [S75], and Maghazi has narrow alleys [S75].
- Buildings are grey reinforced concrete and block, 2-6 storeys (est.), with flat roofs.
  Roofs carry black water tanks and satellite dishes (as on Palestinian roofs generally
  [S74]) and rebar stubs (see section 13).
- On the Israeli side: red-tile-roofed moshav and kibbutz houses among lawns [S78], and
  Tel Aviv's white rendered blocks (est.).
- On the Lebanese coast: dense concrete towns run into the mountain foothills (est.).

**Light.** Humid haze in summer, a milky pale sky near the horizon, and softer shadows
than inland (section 15).

**Military terrain.**
- Movement is easy over open fields, though eased or slowed by irrigation ditches,
  orchards and greenhouses (est.).
- Dunes slow wheeled vehicles but not tracked ones (est.).
- Lines of sight are long over fields, broken by tree rows and ridges. In towns they fall
  to tens of metres.
- Cover comes from orchards, the low kurkar crests (a 20 m ridge dominates a flat plain),
  and buildings.
- Urban terrain dominates: every street is a canyon, every roof a firing post.

## 2. Shephelah lowlands

**Where.** The foothill belt between the coastal plain and the Judean hills.
- The western "Low Shephelah" starts at about 150 m; the eastern "High Shephelah" rises to
  250-450 m [S8].
- Its valleys run east-west: Ayalon, Sorek, Elah, Guvrin and Lachish [S8].

**Climate.** Csa, grading to BSh in the south. About 400-550 mm (est., interpolated between
Beit Dagan's 550 mm [S2] and the northern Negev's 300 mm [S1]).

**Relief and landforms.**
- Rounded, soft chalk hills with broad, flat-floored valleys.
- Relief is 50-200 m over 1-5 km (est., from the 150-450 m range [S8]).
- Valley floors have deep, fertile soil [S9].
- **Tells** are prominent: Azekah, the large mound of Tell Zakariyeh, overlooks the Elah
  valley [S9]. Tells are flat-topped truncated cones [S81]. Heights run from about 10 m
  (Jericho) to more than 40 m [S81].
- Underground: bell-shaped chalk caves and quarries (est.).

**Geology and colour.**
- Soft Eocene chalk (*kirton*) under a hard calcrete crust called **nari** [S8].
- Nari is whitish caliche [S72]. Chalk is near-white to pale cream. On slopes where the
  crust is exposed, the ground reads as patchy white and grey (est.).

**Soils.**
- Rendzinas on chalk and marl are common in the foothills [S10]. They are grey-brown and
  calcareous (est.).
- Brown alluvium lies in the valleys.

**Vegetation.**
- Batha and garrigue on the nari slopes. Low *Sarcopoterium spinosum* dwarf shrubs, up to
  about 50 cm, dominate [S63].
- Scattered *Pistacia lentiscus* (up to 5 m [S63]), carob and terebinth. "Elah" means
  terebinth [S9].
- Planted Aleppo pine forests.
- Fields of wheat and vineyards in the valleys (est.).
- Cover: 30-70% on slopes in winter (est.). Summer is grey-brown shrubs on white ground.

**Water.** Seasonal streams (*nahal*) in the valley floors and cisterns. There are no
perennial rivers (est.).

**Human landscape.**
- Moshavim and kibbutzim with orchards (est.).
- Old Arab villages on hill brows with cactus hedges [S65].
- Ancient terraces on the steeper eastern edge (est.).

**Military terrain.**
- This is the classic approach corridor to the hills: the valleys channel movement east.
- Hill brows and tells give observation over the valley floors.
- Mostly passable to vehicles, apart from nari ledges and stone walls (est.).

## 3. Central hills

The limestone spine of the Levant. It has the same grammar everywhere: grey karst
limestone, red earth in the joints, terraces, olive groves, and villages on spurs. The
heights and the greenness change from north to south.

### 3a. Judea and Samaria

**Where and relief.**
- The Judean mountains average about 900 m and peak at 1,026 m (Halhul) [S13]. Samaria
  rarely exceeds 800 m [S1].
- The **eastern drop** is the most dramatic relief in the region. The ground falls from
  about 800 m at Jerusalem to about −400 m at the Dead Sea within about 25 km [S12], so
  about 1,200 m.
- Relief on the hill country itself is 100-300 m over 1-5 km (est.).
- Steep V-valleys, wadis and karst. The east side is cut by canyons into the Judean Desert
  [S12].

**Climate.**
- Csa on the crest. Jerusalem gets 525 mm [S2]; snow falls in some winters [S2].
- The Judean Desert gets 50-100 mm [S12].

**Geology and colour.**
- Hard Cenomanian-Turonian limestone and dolomite [S10]. Fresh stone is cream and white.
  Weathered outcrops are grey, lichen-spotted pale grey (est.).
- Jerusalem stone varieties [S72]:
  - meleke: white, turning yellow with exposure;
  - mizzi hilu: whitish;
  - mizzi ahmar: light with reddish bands;
  - mizzi yahudi: dark grey or yellow.
  Quarried stone overall ranges "from white to pink, yellow and tawny" [S72].
- The Judean Desert is soft Senonian chalk and marl: pale buff and cream badlands (est.).

**Soils.**
- **Terra rossa** covers the vast majority of the Judean hills, on hard limestone and
  dolomite [S10]. Its colours range between red and brown [S10]. In Munsell terms that is
  a red-to-yellow-red hue, 2.5YR to 5YR [S11][S88].
- Rendzina on chalk [S10].
- Soil sits only in joints and pockets between bare rock "pavements". This rock-and-red
  mosaic is the signature ground (est.).

**Vegetation.**
- Potential cover is evergreen oak maquis. *Quercus calliprinos* dominated the ancient
  woodland [S63], with *Pistacia lentiscus*, terebinth, carob and *Phillyrea*. Today it is
  mostly degraded to garrigue and batha [S63].
- **Planted Aleppo pine** (*Pinus halepensis*) forests:
  - In 1960 about 85% of trees planted were pine [S64]. Today 55-65% of saplings are
    native broadleaves [S64].
  - KKL-JNF has planted more than 100,000 ha [S64].
  - Yatir, about 3,000-3,800 ha and about 90% Aleppo pine, reaches the desert edge
    [S64].
- **Olive terraces** cover the slopes around villages.
- Heights:
  - maquis 2-5 m (est.);
  - batha up to 50 cm [S63];
  - olive 4-8 m (est.);
  - pine 8-15 m (est.).
- Cover:
  - maquis 60-100%;
  - garrigue 30-60%;
  - batha 20-50% shrubs plus winter annuals;
  - rock outcrop 20-60% of the surface on hard limestone (all est.).

**Water.**
- Karst springs (*ein*) at contacts between rock layers, often with a pool and a few
  trees (est.).
- Cisterns.
- Stream beds dry except after storms.

**Human landscape.**
- **Terraces** everywhere on the slopes, dry-laid with no mortar [S80]. One older survey
  says up to 60% of the land was terraced; that is weakly sourced [S80]. Terraces became
  dominant in classical times [S80].
- **Villages on hilltops and spurs.** Cubic flat-roofed stone and concrete houses. Each
  roof carries black water tanks [S74], and Israeli roofs carry solar water heaters [S73].
- Jerusalem itself is stone-faced by a 1918 bylaw [S72].
- Israeli settlements on hilltops have red-tile roofs and ring roads (est.).

**Military terrain.**
- Terraces are a staircase: about 1-3 m risers that stop vehicles off the tracks
  (wall heights are est.; [S80] has no measurement).
- Roads follow ridges and valley floors. Every hill brow overlooks the next valley.
- Stone walls and terraces give excellent infantry cover, and karst caves give
  concealment.
- Lines of sight are long across valleys and short within the olive groves.

### 3b. Galilee

- **Upper Galilee** is a high massif cut by steep valleys. Mount Meron reaches 1,208 m
  [S14]. Rainfall is 900-1,200 mm [S14]; Safed gets 655 mm [S2]. Some snow.
- **Lower Galilee** peaks at 598 m (Kamon) and 588 m (Tabor). Its western hills of
  200-300 m carry oak forests [S14].
- Basalt appears in the **east** of Lower Galilee, near the Golan, with very fertile soils
  [S14]. That gives black stone walls and dark soils beside white limestone.
- The **Tabor oak** (*Quercus ithaburensis*) forms open park woodland in Lower Galilee and
  the Golan. This is my general knowledge; it was not confirmed in the sources found.
- It is greener and more wooded than Judea. It has a winter green flush, wheat and
  pasture valleys, and more maquis (est.).

### 3c. Mount Lebanon (range)

- About 170 km long. It averages above 2,500 m on its high crest, and Qurnat as Sawda'
  reaches 3,088 m [S15].
- Snow averages about 4 m deep on the range [S15]. Snow covers ground above about
  1,500 m from December to April, a figure from a weaker source [S51].
- Oak and pine forests lower down, with **cedar groves on the high slopes** [S15] (see
  section 10).
- Many **abandoned terraces** degraded into thorny garrigue [S54].

### 3d. Anti-Lebanon

- About 150 km long. Mount Hermon is 2,814 m and Ta'la't Musa 2,669 m [S16].
- Jurassic limestone and chalk [S16]. The peaks are snow-covered much of the year [S16].
- It casts a rain shadow over the Syrian desert to its east [S16].
- Small deciduous woods and dry conifers on the west side (Cilician fir, cedar, Greek
  juniper). Alpine communities grow above 2,500 m [S16]. Overgrazing has bared the soil
  [S16].
- It looks barer, greyer and drier than Mount Lebanon (est.).

## 4. Jordan Rift Valley

The rift is a trench along the Dead Sea Transform. Its floor falls below sea level, it is
walled by escarpments, and it runs from desert to tropical-oasis climates.

### 4a. Hula Valley

- 177 km² (about 25 by 6-8 km) at about 70 m above sea level [S18]. Walls rise to
  400-900 m: the Golan to the east and the Naftali mountains to the west [S18].
- Rainfall is about 400 mm in the south and about 800 mm in the north. The adjacent
  Anti-Lebanon gets more than 1,500 mm [S18].
- Former Lake Hula was 12-14 km², 1.5-3 m deep, and thick with **papyrus** [S18]. It was
  drained between 1951 and 1958 [S18]. The peat ignited spontaneously afterwards [S18],
  which makes the drained ground **black peat soil** (colour is est.).
- **Agamon Hula**, re-flooded in the 1990s, is about 1 km² of open water [S18][S19]. It
  hosts tens of thousands of wintering cranes [S18]; about 10,600 were counted at the
  start of one autumn season [S19].
- Landscape: flat fields (peanuts and corn [S19]), canals, reed-lined drains, fishponds
  (est.), and the basalt and limestone walls of the valley.

### 4b. Sea of Galilee (Kinneret)

- 166 km², 23 km long, up to 46 m deep [S1].
- Level about 207-213 m below sea level. The upper red line, a full lake, is 208.8 m below
  sea level; the lower red line is 213 m below; a recent reading was 212.6 m below
  [S22].
- Basalt shores on the north and east, black boulders at the waterline, and pale exposed
  shoreline bands when the level is low (est.).
- Surroundings: date palms, bananas and mango in the hot basin (est.), Tiberias on its
  basalt slopes, and the Golan escarpment rising about 500 m to the east [S24].

### 4c. The Jordan valley (Ghor)

- The Jordan runs about 320 km by its meanders [S1]. Its valley floor is −200 to −400 m.
  It is a hot BWh/BSh climate (est.).
- Jericho gets roughly 120-300 mm. Sources conflict, and 140 mm "in a few violent
  downpours" is the most specific figure [S23].
- **Cross-section from the river outward** (Arabic terms *zor*, *katar*, *ghor*; this is
  my general knowledge and was not confirmed in the sources found):
  1. The *zor*: a narrow, dense riverine thicket of tamarisk, *Populus euphratica*, giant
     reed (*Arundo*) and *Phragmites*.
  2. The *katar*: a band of pale grey-white **Lisan marl badlands**, crumbling and
     gullied.
  3. The *ghor* terrace: irrigated date palms, bananas, vegetables under plastic, and
     fishponds.
  4. The rift escarpments rise 1,000 m or more on the Jordanian side [S44].
- Euphrates-poplar, tamarisk and *Phragmites* thickets are documented on the Euphrates,
  the same riverine community [S49].

### 4d. Dead Sea

- Surface **439.8 m below sea level (2025)**, falling by about 1 m a year [S20]. It is the
  lowest land surface on Earth.
- Rainfall under 50 mm (Sedom 41.9 mm) [S20]. BWh [S20]. July mean maximum 39.7 °C, and
  192 days a year above 30 °C [S20].
- Landforms:
  - **Mount Sodom**, a 210 m halite ridge [S20];
  - the Lisan salt peninsula [S20];
  - **halite-cemented pebble beaches** and teepee halite crusts [S20];
  - **sinkholes** along the receding shore [S20];
  - stair-stepped exposed shore terraces left by the falling level (est.);
  - great alluvial fans at canyon mouths (est.).
- Oases: **Ein Gedi**, historically known for date palms [S20], is a spring-fed green
  wedge against bare cliffs.
- Colours: white salt; pale grey-cream marl; ochre and tawny limestone cliffs; and
  turquoise to steel-blue water, with a white salt rim (est.).

### 4e. The Arava (Arabah)

- 166 km from the Gulf of Aqaba to the Dead Sea [S21]. Its watershed is about 230 m above
  sea level [S21].
- Rainfall about 50 mm [S1], virtually rainless in the south [S21].
- A flat valley floor between the Edom (Jordan) and Negev escarpments.
  - Huge **alluvial fans** spill gravel from side canyons (est.).
  - **Sabkhas** (salt flats) at Yotvata and Evrona-type sites are my general knowledge;
    none was found in the sources.
  - Acacia savanna in the wadis: *A. raddiana*, *A. tortilis*, *A. pachyceras* [S37].
- Kibbutz and moshav oases: about 20 small settlements north of Eilat [S21]. Date palm
  plantations [S21], net-houses and greenhouses.

### Rift military terrain (est.)

- The floor is open, flat and fully observed from both escarpments. It is a killing
  ground.
- Crossings are limited to fords and bridges over the Jordan. The marl badlands and the
  zor thicket stop vehicles beside the river.
- The escarpments are passable only on a few switchback roads. Fans and wadis give the
  only cover on the floor.

## 5. Golan Heights and Hauran

### 5a. Golan Heights

**Where and relief.**
- About 1,800 km² [S24]. The plateau averages about 1,000 m. It rises north towards Hermon
  (2,814 m) and slopes to about 400 m at the Yarmouk in the south [S24].
- The western edge is an escarpment that drops about 500 m to the Jordan valley and the
  Sea of Galilee [S24].
- The north is rugged; the south is more level [S24].
- Relief on the plateau is low (20-100 m over 1-5 km, est.), punctuated by **volcanic
  cones**: Avital 1,204 m, Bental 1,171 m, Dov 1,529 m [S24]. Each cone rises 100-300 m
  above the plateau (est.).
- Deep gorges cut the edges: the Yarmouk in the south [S24] and canyon streams such as the
  Yehudiya [S24].

**Climate.**
- Csa. The north gets about double the rainfall of the south [S25]. The south is given as
  about 250 mm [S25], which seems low; other figures range up to about 600 mm (est.).
- Snow in the north [S25]. Mount Hermon has seasonal snow on all three peaks in winter and
  spring [S2].

**Geology and colour.**
- Basalt flows began about 4 Ma [S24]. Dark, fertile, deep grumusol soils [S25].
- **Basalt boulders cover the north** [S25].
- Under the basalt lie lighter limestones and marls, exposed along the Yarmouk [S24]. The
  Hermon foothills are light Jurassic limestone with karst springs [S24].
- Basalt is charcoal-grey when fresh. It weathers to dark brown-grey, often with pale
  grey-green lichen, and reads near-black when wet (est.).

**Soils.** Basaltic dark-brown grumusols (vertisols) that swell and shrink, with deep
cracks in summer [S29].

**Vegetation.**
- A savanna-like landscape: dense herbaceous growth with sparse trees in the central Golan
  [S25].
- Brush, forest and pasture in the wetter north [S25]. Forest remnants survive near Odem,
  Mount Avital and Yehudiya [S25]. Tabor oak park forest in the south-west is general
  knowledge (see 3b).
- **The season defines the look.** Bright green grass with spring flowers from February to
  April, then golden-tawny dry grass from May to November. Black boulders show through the
  grass all year (est.).
- Cattle grazing is everywhere (est.).

**Water.**
- Birkat Ram crater lake [S24].
- Banias spring and waterfall [S24].
- Many perennial streams cut canyons with waterfalls into the escarpment (est.).

**Human landscape.**
- Basalt-built Druze villages in the north (Majdal Shams) [S24].
- Ruins of abandoned basalt villages, with cactus hedges [S65].
- Israeli moshavim, vineyards and orchards in the north (est.).
- Minefield fences, bunkers and old Syrian trench lines (est.).

**Military terrain.**
- The Golan is "not ideal for mechanized warfare" [S26]. Basalt rock fields channel any
  advance onto "the few roads and tracks" [S26].
- Basalt boulders "chewed through tank treads" in 1967 [S26].
- Volcanic cones are commanding observation points. Mount Bental overlooked one of the
  largest tank battles in history in 1973 [S26].
- The escarpment is a natural wall facing west. The south is more open and "more
  forgiving" [S26].

### 5b. Hauran (southern Syria and northern Jordan)

**Where.** Bounded by the Ghouta to the north, the Golan to the west and the Harrat
al-Sham to the east and south [S27].

**Relief.** The Hauran contains several distinct basalt landscapes:
- **Nuqrah plain:** about 100 by 75 km at about 600 m [S27].
- **Jaydur:** 600-900 m, with cones above 1,000 m [S27].
- **Lajat (Leja):** a lava field at 600-700 m, with cones above 1,150 m, rocky and nearly
  barren [S27].
- **Jabal al-Druze (Jabal Hauran):** a massif of about 60 by 30 km rising above 1,800 m
  [S27].

**Climate.** Above 200 mm everywhere and about 250 mm on the plain, with considerably
more on Jabal Hauran [S27]. BSk/Csa (est.).

**Surface.**
- Arable, basalt-derived soils on the plain [S27], dark red-brown to chocolate (est.).
- A wheat and barley breadbasket [S27]. Wheat is green in winter and golden in May, on
  dark soil (est.).
- The Lajat is rough lava: a labyrinth of black rock, crevasses and pockets of soil (est.).

**Buildings.**
- Basalt is the exclusive vernacular material [S27].
- Roofs are **corbelled flat roofs** of long basalt slabs on corbels [S28]. Arches carry
  the slab spans [S28].
- Umm el-Jimal was built entirely of basalt [S28]. Bosra has a Roman theatre [S27].
- Black towns with stone doors. The doors are reported but not confirmed in detail in the
  sources found [S28].

**Military terrain (est.).**
- The plain is open and fast: tank country with long lines of sight, broken only by cones
  and tells.
- The Lajat is near-impassable to vehicles and ideal for infantry hiding. It has been a
  refuge region throughout history; that last point is my general knowledge.

## 6. Negev

### 6a. Northern and central Negev

**Where.** The triangle south of the Hebron hills and the Shephelah. The Negev is
12,000-16,000 km², more than half of Israel; the source gives both figures [S1].

**Climate.**
- Rainfall by sub-region [S1]:
  - northern Negev 300 mm;
  - western Negev 250 mm;
  - central Negev 200 mm;
  - Ramat HaNegev 100 mm (Sede Boker watershed 92 mm [S34]);
  - Arava about 50 mm.
- Beersheba gets 193 mm [S2].
- Köppen class: BSh in the north, BWh further south [S2].
- Rain is highly variable and comes in violent storms that cause **flash floods** [S2].

**Relief and landforms.**
- **Northern Negev (Beersheba basin):** gently rolling loess plains (relief 10-50 m over
  1-5 km, est.).
  - Valleys are filled with fluvial loess up to 20 m thick [S32]. The largest fill is the
    lower Besor [S32].
  - **Loess badlands and deep gullies** have formed over the last 12,000 years [S32], and
    gullying is enhanced on farmed loess [S32].
- **Western Negev:** sandy and partly dune-covered [S1]. The vegetated linear dunes are the
  continuation of the north Sinai erg [S41].
- **Negev Highlands (Ramat HaNegev):** 370-520 m in the regional description [S1], rising
  higher at the Ramon rim (est. about 1,000 m). Stepped limestone plateaus, mesas, cuestas
  and stone-paved slopes (est.).
- **Makhteshim** are erosion cirques: steep walls of resistant rock around a closed
  valley, each drained by a narrow outlet. Ramon is the exception, with two outlets
  [S30][S31].
  - **Makhtesh Ramon** is about 40 km long, 2-10 km wide and about 500 m deep [S30].
  - **HaMakhtesh HaGadol** is 5 by 10 km by one source and 14 by 6 km, 450 m deep, by
    another [S31].
  - **HaMakhtesh HaKatan** is 5 by 7 km, or about 8 by 6 km and about 400 m deep [S31].
  - Their floors expose colourful rock:
    - red and yellow clay hills [S30];
    - black basalt hills (Giv'at Ga'ash) [S30];
    - columnar quartzite ("the Carpentry", HaMinsara) [S30];
    - creamy wall rock [S30];
    - multi-coloured Nubian sandstone [S36].

**Geology and colour.**
- Loess is a 10YR hue (yellow-brown) silt [S33]. A 10YR 6/4 "light yellowish brown"
  sample appears in a related coastal dataset [S33].
- Limestone and chalk plateaus are pale grey-cream.
- **Desert pavement (hamada and reg)** covers the stony plains: a closely packed surface
  of stones [S39], often with dark brown to black **desert varnish** [S39]. Varnish holds
  about 100 times more manganese than the surrounding dust [S39]. A varnished hamada reads
  as a dark brown-grey sheet of chips with paler ground between (est.).
- Flint scatters are typical (est.).

**Soils.**
- Loess (loessial serozem) in the north (est. naming).
- Light, partly sandy soils in the west [S1].
- Impervious soils in the centre that cause runoff [S1].
- Partly salty soils on the high plateau [S1].

**Vegetation.**
- A rainfall gradient with three study sites [S34]:
  - Matta, 540 mm;
  - Lahav, 300 mm, mostly *Sarcopoterium spinosum*;
  - Sede Boker, 90 mm, dominated by *Zygophyllum dumosum*.
- Sede Boker watershed cover [S34]:
  - 10-30% perennial cover on rocky upper slopes;
  - less than 10% on the lower colluvium, which is true desert;
  - perennials include *Artemisia herba-alba*, *Hammada scoparia*, *Noaea*, *Reaumuria*
    and *Zygophyllum*. *Hammada* leads on the loess of the wadis [S34].
- Shrubs are 20-60 cm, dark grey-green to brownish (est.).
- After a good winter the loess plains flush green for 6-10 weeks, with red anemones in
  the north (est.).
- Planted forests stretch to the desert edge: Yatir [S64], and "savannization" plantings
  of sparse trees on contour ridges [S64].
- Acacia and *Ziziphus* grow in large wadis (est.).

**Water.**
- Dry wadis with **flash floods** [S2]. Wadi floors are braided gravel channels with
  scattered tamarisk and acacia (est.).
- Cisterns. Ancient runoff farms: the Nabataean/Byzantine terraced wadis at Avdat and
  Shivta, with check dams and stone conduits on the hillsides [S38].

**Human landscape.**
- Bedouin settlements: seven recognised towns plus 35-45 unrecognised villages of
  tin-and-block shacks without full services [S77]. The black goat-hair tent is now rare;
  the *shig* guest tent is still in use [S77].
- Development towns of concrete blocks (est.).
- Kibbutzim with green lawns as islands.
- Military firing zones and long straight roads (est.).

**Military terrain (est.).**
- The loess plains are open and fast when dry, but **gullies 2-10 m deep** are anti-tank
  obstacles. The loess turns to clinging mud after rain.
- The Highlands' stepped cliffs and makhtesh walls restrict vehicles to a few passes.
- Very long lines of sight. Little cover except gullies, wadi banks and boulder fields.

### 6b. Southern Negev and the Eilat mountains

- The Precambrian basement is schist, gneiss and several granites, cut by a **dike swarm**
  [S35]:
  - dikes cover about 10% of the exposed area on average and more than 50% locally [S35];
  - the dikes are basic to intermediate and dark [S35].
- Sedimentary cover above the basement: Nubian sandstone, then limestone. The ensemble is
  described as "extremely colorful" [S35].
- **Timna** shows predominantly red Nubian sandstone, with yellow, orange, grey, dark brown
  and black, and light green or blue near the copper [S36]. Solomon's Pillars are reddish
  sandstone cliffs with a hard red rind [S36].
- Rainfall about 24-30 mm [S1][S2]. Rocks bare of all vegetation except acacias in wadis
  [S37].
- Relief is sharp: 300-800 m over 1-3 km from the Eilat mountains to the Gulf (est.).

**Military terrain (est.).** Granite massifs are impassable except by wadis, which make
natural ambush corridors.

## 7. Sinai

About 60,000 km² [S40]. Three belts run from north to south [S40].

### 7a. North coast and dune belt

- Flat sand with **long parallel dune lines, some over 100 m high**, between the coast and
  dome-shaped hills [S40].
  - Active linear (seif) dunes in north Sinai continue as vegetated linear dunes in the
    north-west Negev [S41].
  - Near El Arish, transverse dunes dominate [S41].
- Rainfall: 73 mm at Port Said rising to about 105 mm at El Arish, from 1989-1999 station
  data [S41]. Other sources say under 200 mm [S41]. Köppen BWh (est.).
- El Arish has about 160,000 people [S40].
- Coastal lagoon: the Bardawil sabkha and lagoon is my general knowledge.
- Cactus pear is grown, and cactus hedges were part of defensive positions in World War I
  [S40].
- Colour: pale yellow-cream dune sand. Date palms dot the El Arish coast (est.).
- **Military terrain:** dune belts are difficult for wheels and slow for armour, which
  channels movement onto the coast road and the few inland tracks (est.).

### 7b. Central plateau (el-Tih)

- A calcareous (limestone) plateau [S40], with stony hamada surfaces, wide shallow wadis
  and north-facing escarpments (est.).
- **Mitla** is a 480 m-high pass 32 km long, between ranges [S43]. Mitla and Giddi are the
  main east-west passages through the central highlands. They were blocked as bottlenecks
  in 1967 and fought over in 1973 [S43].
- Colour: buff-grey limestone with dark varnished gravel and pale wadi floors (est.).

### 7c. Southern granite mountains

- Granite mountains around St Catherine [S40]. Mount Catherine is Egypt's highest point:
  2,642 m by Britannica, 2,629 m by another source [S40][S42].
- Rainfall: under 50 mm in most of Sinai, and up to about 100 mm at the high mountains
  [S42].
- Pink, red and grey granite, with dark dikes (as at Eilat). Wadis floored with coarse
  granite gravel (est.).
- Very sparse vegetation. Acacia in wadis. Bedouin walled gardens with almond and olive in
  the high basins (general knowledge).
- **Military terrain:** the south is "all but impassable to armies" [S43]. The few wadi
  roads are the only routes.

## 8. Jordanian plateau, Wadi Rum, Harra

### 8a. Jordanian highlands and plateau

- About 300 km long. Peaks run from more than 1,200 m in the north to 1,700 m at Jebel
  Mubarak in the south [S44].
- The western edge drops **1,000 m or more** to the Jordan rift [S44].
- East-west wadis cut the escarpment: the Zarqa and Mujib, draining to the Jordan and the
  Dead Sea [S44].
- Rainfall is about 600 mm in the northern mountains, falling to about 50 mm in the far
  south and on the eastern plateau [S44].
- Vegetation [S44]:
  - Aleppo pine and evergreen oak in the north (Ajloun, Dibeen);
  - oak, cypress, juniper and pistachio further south;
  - juniper on sandstone near Dana.
- The east is a flat, stony desert plateau.
- **Military terrain (est.):** escarpment wadis are deep obstacles that run across a
  north-south advance. The plateau beyond is open and fast.

### 8b. Wadi Rum

- 720 km², about 60 km east of Aqaba [S45]. Elevation 45-1,750 m in its infobox [S45]. Jabal
  Umm ad Dami is 1,840-1,854 m and Jabal Ram 1,734 m [S45].
- **Geology:**
  - Near-vertical mountains of iron-rich Umm Ishrin sandstone [S45];
  - flat-floored valleys of alluvium, aeolian sand and **salt pans** between them [S45];
  - arkosic sandstone and a granite basement (the Aqaba complex) below [S45];
  - springs at the contact [S45].
- Sand and rock are red from iron oxide [S86]. In the southern wilderness the reds give
  way to gold [S86].
- Rainfall is 74 mm, from thunderstorms that cause flash floods [S45].
- Landforms [S45]:
  - natural bridges;
  - tafoni;
  - narrow canyons;
  - barchans, climbing dunes (sand ramps to hilltops) and echo dunes.
- Vegetation is scant shrub-steppe of *Haloxylon*, *Anabasis*, *Retama*, *Artemisia* and
  *Acacia tortilis* bushes. Acacia and juniper grow along seepage lines at the cliff feet
  [S45].
- Relief: the jebels rise 300-800 m sheer above valley floors 1-3 km wide (est.).
- **Military terrain (est.):** valley floors are open sand corridors, fast for 4x4s and
  soft for heavy vehicles. The massifs are impassable walls with long lines of sight along
  the corridors.

### 8c. The eastern basalt desert (Harrat al-Sham, the "Black Desert")

- About 40,000 km² across Syria, Jordan and Saudi Arabia; the figure is marked as needing
  a citation [S46]. More than 800 cones and about 140 dikes [S46].
- Some eruptions are Holocene, and there was historic activity [S46].
- The surface is rocky basalt desert, dense with boulders [S46].
  - The dense basalt makes travel "extremely difficult, even for modern vehicles" [S46].
  - Muddy **qa' playas** (seasonal mudflats) and wadis are the only natural access
    points [S46].
  - Modern tracks are made by bulldozers pushing the boulders aside. That is a traveller's
    anecdote [S46].
- Qa' Shubayqa floods after winter rain and is cropped [S46].
- Vegetation: open acacia shrubland, with juniper patches higher up (Saudi portion)
  [S46].
- Human marks [S46]:
  - **desert kites** (stone-walled game traps);
  - cairns;
  - desert castles (Qasr Burqu', Qasr al-Azraq);
  - Bedouin grazing;
  - Safawi, built around a Kirkuk-Haifa pipeline pump station.
- Colour: black-brown boulders over paler buff silt. The qa' floors are smooth pale grey
  and cream (est.).
- **Military terrain:** vehicles are confined to cleared tracks and qa' flats. Infantry
  can move anywhere, slowly. The ground is flat with long lines of sight and almost no
  cover above boulder height (est.).

## 9. Syria

### 9a. The Badia (Syrian steppe and desert)

- It covers 55% of Syria and 85% of Jordan (secondary sources quoting FAO) [S47]. It is
  defined by less than 200 mm of rain [S47]. Palmyra gets 127 mm, averaged over 42 years
  [S47].
- Vegetation is dwarf shrubs, primarily *Artemisia herba-alba*, with annuals in good years
  [S47]. Cover is 5-25% (est.). It is grey-green to buff, rolling and gravel-strewn.
- Relief: broad plateaus and low ridges, the Palmyrene chains rising 300-600 m above the
  plain, and wadis (est.).
- Colour: buff-grey gravel and silt, chalky-white limestone outcrops, and some gypsum
  crusts (est.).
- **Military terrain:** vast and open, with long lines of sight. Fast for wheels when dry.
  Cover only in wadis and on ridges (est.).

### 9b. Euphrates valley

- The valley is 80-250 m below the surrounding plains and 2-12 km wide [S49].
- Riverine thickets of *Populus euphratica*, *Tamarix*, *Salix* and *Typha* [S49].
- Irrigated "mazara": cotton and cereals, with orchards and plantations of poplar and
  Aleppo pine [S49]. Between Raqqa and the Iraqi border the river flows through steppe
  [S49].
- The Tabqa dam created Lake Assad, which drowned floodplain [S49].
- Deir ez-Zor gets 156-183 mm, BWh [S48].
- The look is a **green ribbon** between pale bluffs. The edge between the two is abrupt,
  as on the Nile (section 12) (est.).
- Mud-brick and concrete villages line the terrace edges. Beehive mud-brick houses also
  occur in northern Syria (general knowledge; see section 11).

### 9c. Aleppo and Homs plains; the Orontes

- Aleppo gets about 353 mm [S48] and Homs about 396 mm [S48]. Classifications conflict
  between Csa, BSk and BWh [S48].
- Rolling red-brown plains of wheat, olive and pistachio around Aleppo (general knowledge;
  not confirmed in the sources found). Aleppo is a limestone city (general knowledge).
- The **Ghab** is the Orontes depression, 63-90 km long, 8-12 km wide and 170-200 m above
  sea level [S50]. It was a malarial swamp, drained from 1953 and entirely by 1968, adding
  about 41,000 ha of irrigated land [S50]. Now a flat grid of fields under the Jebel
  Ansariyah wall (est.).

## 10. Lebanon

- **Coast:** see section 1. Beirut is Csa, 700-900 mm [S52].
- **Mount Lebanon:**
  - Qurnat as Sawda' is 3,088 m, with snow about 4 m deep [S15].
  - Terraces climb the slopes, many abandoned [S54].
  - Planted stone pine (*Pinus pinea*) covers about 12,740 ha, on non-alkaline sandy soils
    between Jezzine and Metn [S54].
  - Oak and pine woodland lies below the cedars [S15].
- **Cedars (*Cedrus libani*):**
  - Natural Lebanese range figures disagree: 1,200-1,900 m, 1,200-1,800 m, or 1,500-1,700 m
    [S17]. A northern-Lebanon dataset sampled the limits at 1,232 m and 2,011 m [S17].
    The range is 1,300-3,000 m across Lebanon and Turkey together [S17].
  - Precipitation: 1,000-1,500 mm [S17].
  - **Cedars are high-mountain trees and only survive as relict groves**, in the Cedars of
    God (Bsharri) and Shouf reserves [S15]. A broad, tiered, flat-topped crown when old
    (general knowledge).
- **Villages:** the central-hall house, a stone cube with a **red-tile pyramid roof** and a
  triple-arched façade [S53]. The tile dates to the 19th century [S53]. Built of
  limestone, sandstone and basalt [S53]. This is the one sub-region where red-tile roofs
  are vernacular rather than modern.
- **Beqaa:**
  - Floor at 900-1,000 m between the two ranges [S51].
  - Zahle gets about 600 mm; the northern Beqaa near Hermel gets about 230 mm. Another
    source gives 400-600 mm for Zahle and Baalbek [S51]. Transitional Csa/BSk [S51].
  - About 40% of Lebanon's arable land, with vineyards [S51]. Irrigated by snowmelt
    [S51].
  - The look is a flat chequer of fields with snow-capped walls on both sides in winter
    and spring. Brown and dusty in the dry north (est.).
- **The south (Jabal Amel):** rolling to hilly limestone, cut by the **Litani gorge**,
  which reaches up to about 275 m deep [S55]. The river lies about 6-28 km north of the
  Israeli border [S55]. Olive, tobacco and maquis on terraces (general knowledge; the
  400-900 m elevation band is est.).

**Military terrain (est.).**
- Mountain Lebanon is a vertical labyrinth: roads hug the contours, villages hold the
  spurs, and gorges like the Litani are major obstacles.
- The Beqaa is open, overlooked from both ranges.
- The south is hill-and-wadi country. Short sight lines in the wadis contrast with long
  ones from the crests. Terraces and stone villages give cover; maquis gives concealment.

## 11. Southern Turkey

- **Çukurova (Cilician plain):** Turkey's largest alluvial plain, built by the Seyhan and
  Ceyhan [S56].
  - Csa. Adana gets about 625-726 mm and Karataş on the coast about 1,019 mm [S56].
  - Cotton, citrus, maize, soy, peanuts and wheat [S56]. About 28% of Turkey's citrus
    [S56].
  - Flat to the horizon, with irrigation canals. Dark alluvium (est.).
- **Amanos (Nur) mountains:** highest point Bozdağ, 2,240 m [S57]. A forested range
  (general knowledge: Turkish pine, *Pinus brutia*, and fir higher up), between the
  Çukurova and the Amik/Antakya basin.
- **Hatay/Antakya:** Csa. Rainfall 658 mm (1991-2021) or 983 mm (WorldClim 1970-2000); the
  figures conflict [S57].
  - The Amik plain was a lake and marsh, drained in the 20th century (general knowledge).
  - Olive groves and red-tile Ottoman-era houses are general knowledge.
- **Harran plain (Şanlıurfa):**
  - Şanlıurfa province gets 458-518 mm [S58]. Harran itself gets 330-380 mm, from a
    low-quality aggregator [S58]. Csa/BSk [S58].
  - Flat, treeless, and once dry-farmed. Now irrigated cotton under the GAP scheme
    [S59].
  - **Beehive houses**: corbelled conical domes of mud brick or burnt brick, quick to
    build [S59]. Each course is set slightly inward over the one below, so the walls close
    to a cone [S59]. Clusters of white-plastered or mud-brown cones (est.).
  - Karacadağ basalt lies to the north-east (general knowledge).

## 12. Nile edges

- **The green-desert line.**
  - The valley is flanked by steep desert plateaus that sharply define its edges [S60].
  - Arable land is a narrow strip, 3-22 km wide in Upper Egypt, depending on the stretch
    [S60].
  - The delta is about 22,000 km², about 160 km from north to south and up to about
    250 km wide at the sea [S60].
- **Kemet and Deshret.** The ancient names were "black land" (the silt) and "red land"
  (the desert) [S60]. That is the palette of the edge: very dark wet silt and intensely
  green fields abutting buff-orange desert, with **no transition zone** where the
  irrigation ends (est.).
- Climate: BWh, about 25 mm at Cairo (general knowledge). All water is the river.
- **Villages** [S61]:
  - A mosque or church, a school, and a **decorated pigeon cote** [S61].
  - Prosperous houses are fired brick with concrete reinforcement, flat-roofed with many
    balconies. Many are left as **unpainted red brick and concrete** [S61].
  - Traditional mud brick with dung-and-straw plaster [S61].
  - Adobe pigeon towers [S61].
- Canals lined with eucalyptus and date palms, and bright green clover fields. These are
  general knowledge.
- **Military terrain (est.):** a canal grid every few hundred metres stops vehicles. Dense
  villages and palm groves limit lines of sight. The desert edge is a sharp line of open
  observation.

## 13. What makes the Levant recognisable

The at-a-glance signatures, ranked by how much each one carries (judgement):

1. **Pale stone and red earth in a mosaic.** Grey-white limestone pavements with terra
   rossa in the joints [S10]. This one image says "Judea / Galilee / Lebanon".
2. **Terraces and dry-stone walls** contouring every hill slope, many of them abandoned and
   overgrown [S80][S54].
3. **Olive groves.** Grey-green, rounded, low (4-8 m, est.), planted in loose grids on
   terraces. They are silvery when the wind turns the leaves (est.).
4. **Flat-roofed cubic buildings.** Each roof carries water tanks [S74], solar heaters on
   Israeli roofs [S73], satellite dishes [S74], and rebar stubs for the next floor.
   Rebar stubs are general knowledge: the reason is often cost staging or permit
   constraints, and I found no source for it.
5. **Seasonal colour.** A green flush from December to April, straw-gold from May to
   November [S2][S67]. Evergreens stay dark against the straw.
6. **Specific planted trees:**
   - dark Aleppo pine forests in rows on hills [S64];
   - eucalyptus rows on the plains [S66];
   - date palms in hot valleys and on the south coast [S89][S21];
   - prickly pear hedges at old villages [S65];
   - cypress spires (general knowledge).
7. **Black basalt** in the north-east: boulder-strewn pasture, black villages, black
   shores [S24][S27].
8. **Desert that is mostly stone, not sand.** Hamada with varnish [S39], loess [S32],
   cliffs and makhteshim [S30]. Dunes are limited to particular belts: the north
   Sinai/western Negev erg [S40][S41], Wadi Rum's red sand ramps [S45], and the coastal
   belts.
9. **The rift.** A flat, hot, below-sea-level floor between huge escarpments, with salt and
   oasis green [S20][S21].
10. **Tells.** Flat-topped mounds rising above valley floors; the Southern Levant has more
    than 5,000 [S81].

## 14. Common mistakes

1. **Sand everywhere.** The Negev, el-Tih and the Badia are mostly gravel pavement, loess,
   limestone ledges and chalk [S1][S39][S47]. Use dunes only where they belong:
   - the north Sinai/NW Negev erg [S41];
   - coastal belts [S5];
   - Wadi Rum [S45].
2. **Wrong trees.**
   - No conifers in the low desert. Pine plantings stop at about the Yatir edge [S64].
   - Cedars only above about 1,200 m on Mount Lebanon [S17], never in the coastal hills.
   - Acacia, tamarisk and *Ziziphus* belong in desert wadis and the rift [S37][S62], not
     in the Mediterranean coastal plain.
   - No broadleaf deciduous forest outside Tabor oak parkland and stream galleries.
   - The desert tree is acacia: flat-topped, sparse and thorny.
3. **Too-saturated green.**
   - Mediterranean evergreens are dark, dusty and grey-green: olive, *Pistacia*, oak.
   - Winter grass is green, but it is a sward of annuals over stony ground, never a
     European lawn (est.).
   - Lush green is right only on irrigated land and in short spring windows.
4. **Wrong rock colour.**
   - The hills are grey-white limestone, not orange.
   - The north-east is near-black basalt.
   - Only the far south is red sandstone and pink granite [S36][S35].
   - The Dead Sea is white salt over pale grey marl [S20].
5. **Missing terraces and stone walls.** Slopes without them look like generic
   Mediterranean scrub [S80].
6. **Buildings without flat roofs and rooftop clutter.** Pitched roofs are wrong except for
   Lebanese red-tile houses [S53], modern Israeli suburbs and kibbutz red-tile houses, and
   Harran's beehive cones [S59].
7. **One stone for all buildings.** Jerusalem is stone-faced [S72]. Gaza, refugee camps and
   most new West Bank and Egyptian building is grey concrete block or red brick and
   concrete [S61].
8. **Uniform haze.** The region swings between razor-clear blue days after winter fronts
   and a yellow-brown dust pall on sharav days (section 15) [S68][S69].

The 09:00 values are local solar time. Day length ignores refraction. All values in the
table are calculated (est.).

Consequences:
- Summer noon shadows are tiny: shadow length is 0.14 × height at 82°. Summer light is
  flat, top-down and glaring.
- Winter noon is a long-shadow light at 30-37°, giving the hills strong modelling.

**Colour temperature.**
- Noon sun is about 5,500 K and sunrise/sunset about 2,800 K [S71]. Overcast skylight is
  7,500-10,000 K [S71].
- Clear-sky daylight, D65, mixes warm direct sun with cool skylight [S71].
- In dusty air the sun's disc whitens and dims at noon, then goes orange-red low in the sky
  much earlier than in clean air (est.).

**Haze and dust.**
- AOD (aerosol optical depth: how much dust and haze dims the sun) peaks in spring and
  summer in the Eastern Mediterranean, driven by Saharan and Middle-Eastern dust [S69].
  Winter AOD is lower [S69].
- Two seasonal maxima, one dust and one anthropogenic [S69].
- Sede Boker's long-term mean AOD at 440 nm is about 0.21 [S69].
- Dust days in Israel increased by about 2.7 days per decade from 1958 to 2006 [S68].
  Dust now arrives year-round [S68].
- **Sharav / hamsin:** hot, dry easterly or southerly desert wind episodes.
  - Mostly spring (March to May) and autumn [S68].
  - They last 2-3 days, and the temperature can jump about 10 °C in hours [S68].
  - The popular "50 days a year" figure is folk etymology, not a measurement [S68].
  - The sky turns beige to yellow-grey, distant ridges vanish within a few km, and shadows
    go soft (est.).

**Sky colour.**
- Clean winter days after a front have a deep blue zenith and crisp horizons over 50 km or
  more. That is when Hermon's snow is visible from Galilee (est.).
- Summer coastal skies are milky and pale at the horizon from humidity and haze (est.).
- Desert skies are a hard, pale blue at the horizon, bleached near the sun (est.).

**Desert light against coastal light (est.).**
- In the desert, high-albedo ground (chalk, loess, salt) bounces light into the shadows,
  so shadows are lighter and warm. Contrast is extreme on varnished hamada and basalt.
- At the coast, humid air softens distant contrast and adds a blue-grey veil. Vegetation
  and buildings read darker and warmer under the haze.
- In the rift, the deep-basin haze over the Dead Sea gives a milky, low-contrast distance
  even on clear days. The escarpments across the water appear as pale silhouettes. This
  is a commonly observed effect, but I found no source for it.
